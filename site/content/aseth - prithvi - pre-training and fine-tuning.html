Part 1

Pre-training: a masked autoencoder on satellite time series

The running example is a synthetic 224 × 224 pixel HLS scene (6.7 km on a side) seen on four dates: a river with a lake, a town, a forest, and fields of a summer crop, a winter crop and pasture. The river floods in August and a cloud sits over the town in February. Everything below is computed from this scene in your browser.

1.1

One training sample: 4 dates × 6 bands × 224 × 224

HLS harmonises Landsat 8/9 and Sentinel-2 surface reflectance at 30 m. Prithvi uses the six bands both satellites share. A pre-training sample is four images of the same place, taken 1–6 months apart, so it spans seasons. Click a pixel to see its six band values on each date.

Pixel spectra

Vegetation is dark in red and bright in NIR; water is dark in NIR and SWIR; soil rises steadily towards SWIR. The summer and winter crops look alike on some dates and opposite on others, which is why the dates matter.

1.2

3D patch embedding: cubes become tokens

A 3D convolution cuts the sample into non-overlapping cubes of 1 date × p × p pixels and maps each cube, all six bands at once, to one vector: a token. The time depth is 1 because satellite dates are irregular and far apart. Click a patch to see what goes into its token.

Model
Token

Projected token (first 48 of its numbers)

The projection here uses fixed random weights so you can see that different patches give different vectors. In Prithvi the weights are learned during pre-training, a weight tensor of shape (D, 6, 1, p, p).

Inside the patch embedding

It is one 3D convolution layer: 6 input channels (the bands), D output channels (1,024 for the 300M model), kernel 1 × 16 × 16 (date × height × width) and a stride of the same size, with no padding. Because the stride equals the kernel, the windows never overlap: each output position sees exactly one cube. So the layer is simply a linear map. Each of the 1,024 outputs is a weighted sum of all 1,536 numbers in the cube plus a bias, computed with its own set of weights, its filter. The same 1,024 filters are applied to every patch on every date. There is no activation function in this step; the result is flattened into a list of tokens.

Is anything learned here? Yes. The filters start random and are trained by backpropagation together with the encoder and decoder, using the same reconstruction loss. They become detectors for useful spectral-spatial patterns, such as "bright NIR with dark red" or "a dark line through the patch". In fine-tuning they can be updated again or kept frozen.

Six bands, not RGB

Most image transformers are pre-trained on RGB photographs. Prithvi was pre-trained on the six HLS bands from the start. Only this first layer knows how many bands there are: its filters mix every band of every pixel in a cube into each of the token's numbers. Every transformer block after it sees only D-number tokens, so the band count matters here and nowhere else.

  • Other sensors: feed the six matching bands, as the paper does for Sentinel-2. Extra bands such as red-edge, or radar, need new input channels in this layer, and those start untrained.
  • RGB-only data: the benchmark gave each model only the bands it was pre-trained on, so high-resolution RGB tasks used three of Prithvi's six inputs.
  • How other models handle new bands: copying RGB filters into new channels, or, in DOFA, generating the filters from each band's wavelength.
1.3

Position encodings: telling tokens where they are

Attention treats tokens as an unordered set, so each token gets a fixed "where am I" vector added to it. Each position becomes a set of sine and cosine waves of different wavelengths. Dimension pair i uses angular frequency 1 / base2i/D, with base = 10,000. Here D is the number of dimensions given to one axis, not 3: in the released code the token width is split roughly ¼ to time, ⅜ to rows and ⅜ to columns, and the three encodings are concatenated.

One encoding dimension, painted on the tiles

Each token's position code is a list of numbers. Pick one of them and colour every patch by its value: blue for −1, orange for +1. Some dimensions flip between neighbouring patches; others change slowly across the whole tile. The column code varies only across x, the row code only across y, and the date code only between the four tiles.

Which patches the encodings tie together

Attention compares tokens by dot products, so two tokens whose position codes are similar find it easy to attend to each other. Click a patch to make it the reference; every patch is shaded by how similar its full x, y, t code is to the reference's, using only the waves you choose.

Why the 10,000 in base2i/D

The term sets each pair's wavelength: 2π × base2i/D positions. Wavelengths grow geometrically from about 6 positions for pair 0 to about 63,000 for the last pair, like the digits of a number: fast waves resolve neighbours, slow waves give coarse location without repeating. A base much larger than any sequence length guarantees the slowest waves never wrap around. On a 14-patch grid most pairs are slow and nearly constant, which is harmless but means only a few dozen dimensions do the local work.

Where on Earth, and when: metadata encodings (the "-TL" models)

  • From numbers to vectors. Each value is treated like a position and given the same sine/cosine code. Latitude gets D/2 numbers (512 for the 300M model) and longitude another D/2; concatenated, they form one D-number location vector. Year and day of year are encoded the same way into one D-number date vector. The paper does not give units; the raw values (degrees, the year, day 1–365) appear to be used directly.
  • Into the tokens. Each vector is multiplied by one learned number, wL for location and wT for date, and added to every token like a bias. The position code tells a token where it sits inside the sample; the metadata tells the whole sample where and when it is.
  • What this simple scheme can and cannot say. The fastest wave repeats every 6.3 units, so places a few kilometres apart get almost identical codes: the model learns broad geography and season, not exact sites. Day 365 and day 1 get very different codes although they are one day apart.
  • Dropping. Each was left out 10% of the time during pre-training, so the model also works when a date or location is unknown.

1.4

Masking: what is hidden, and what clues remain

Prithvi hides a random subset of tokens drawn from all dates and positions together. A hidden token loses all six bands at once, like a cloud. The paper does not state the ratio; the released default is 75%, as in the original MAE. Compare Prithvi's choice with three alternatives it did not use. The statistics show which kinds of clue a hidden token still has. Masking has no parameters and learns nothing: it only decides what the encoder sees and where the loss is computed. That choice still shapes what the encoder learns.

1.5

The ViT encoder: tokens attend to each other

A Vision Transformer is a stack of identical blocks (24 in the 300M model, 32 in the 600M). In each block, every token makes a query ("what am I looking for"), a key ("what do I contain") and a value ("what I pass on"). Each token compares its query with all keys, turns the scores into weights with a softmax, and takes the weighted mix of values. Several heads do this in parallel, each free to learn a different relationship; a small feed-forward network then processes each token. Unlike a CNN, any token can reach any other in the first layer. Click a patch to make it the query.

Is this where the learning happens? Mostly: the encoder holds about 300 M of the 300M model's parameters, and it is the part reused for every downstream task. But training is end to end: a single reconstruction loss updates the patch embedding (1.2), the metadata weights (1.3), the encoder (1.5) and the decoder (1.6) together. Section 1.7 lists what is learned.

Head behaviours here are illustrative, built from rules: the spatial head prefers nearby patches on the same date, the temporal head the same place on other dates, and the look-alike head patches whose average spectra resemble the query's, computed from this scene. Real heads learn their own mixtures.

1.6

Decoder and loss: rebuild the hidden pixels

A lighter transformer decoder receives the encoded visible tokens plus a learned "mask token" in each hidden slot, with position encodings so it knows which slot is which. It predicts the six-band pixels of every hidden patch. The loss is the mean squared error on hidden patches only. Drag training progress to see predictions improve; the masks come from section 1.4.

Decoder architecture

MAE's decoder is deliberately lighter than the encoder because it is thrown away after pre-training. It first projects the visible tokens to its own, smaller width, then puts a copy of one learned mask token in every hidden slot so the sequence is complete again. Position codes are added so each mask token knows which slot it fills, then a few transformer blocks let the mask tokens gather information from the visible ones. A final linear layer turns each token into the pixel values of its cube. The shapes below follow your current model and mask.

Part 2

Fine-tuning for downstream tasks

The paper fine-tuned Prithvi-EO-2.0 on nine applications plus the twelve GEO-Bench datasets, each on a single GPU. They fall into six recipes. Answer four questions to find yours, then try the matching playground.

At inference the encoder sees every token (nothing is masked), inputs get the same band order and normalisation as in training, and for maps the highest-scoring class is taken at each pixel. Large areas are cut into chips of the training size and the predictions stitched back together; this is standard practice, and the paper does not describe its own tiling.

2.1

Three ways to adapt the encoder

  • Full fine-tuning: every weight, encoder included, is updated with a small learning rate. Used for floods, burn scars, landslides and US crops.
  • LoRA: the encoder's weights stay frozen; small low-rank corrections are trained instead. Used for burn severity, PASTIS and biomass.
  • Frozen encoder: no encoder weights change; only the task head learns from the embeddings. Used for GPP.

LoRA: fine-tuning a small fraction of the parameters

Low-Rank Adaptation freezes each pretrained weight matrix W₀ and learns a correction ΔW = B·A of low rank r. A (r × k) starts with small random values; B (d × r) starts at zero, so ΔW = 0 and training begins exactly at the pretrained model. They cannot both start at zero: each one's gradient depends on the other, so neither would ever move. The final weight is one ordinary matrix, W′ = W₀ + (α / r) · B·A, which can be merged once so inference costs nothing extra.

The small matrices are an 8 × 8 illustration with rank up to 4; the counts above are for the real sizes. LoRA usually adapts the attention projections; the task decoder is still trained in full.

2.2

From your images to a per-pixel map: sizes and shapes

Fine-tuning data rarely looks like the pre-training samples: chips are bigger or smaller, there are 1 or 20 dates instead of 4, and pixels may be 10 m or 1 m instead of 30 m. This calculator shows what the model does with your input and what each stage of a segmentation model passes on, which is the information the R1–R4 tables below summarise for the paper's tasks.

  • Image size: any height and width work if they are multiples of the patch size (16 or 14 px). Position codes are computed for whatever grid results, so nothing needs retraining.
  • Number of dates: the time code extends the same way. More dates mean more tokens, and attention cost grows with the square of the token count.
  • Pixel size: the model has no notion of metres. At 10 m a 16-pixel patch covers 160 m instead of 480 m; fine-tuning has to absorb that change of scale.
  • What the decoder receives: the encoder's tokens, laid back out as a grid with one vector per patch per date. The dates are merged, a neck builds four resolutions, and the decoder turns them into class scores that are upsampled to every pixel.

Single-date map: floods, burn scars, landslides

What the paper found

How it was trained and run

Floods and burn scars used the same recipe. Each 512 × 512 chip was resized to 448 × 448, so that both patch sizes divide it evenly, and passed through the encoder as a single date. The encoder's output tokens were laid back out on their 28 × 28 grid. A neck took the outputs of four encoder blocks and resized them into a pyramid of four resolutions, so the UPerNet decoder had both broad context and finer detail. UPerNet produced land/water (or unburnt/burnt) scores at a quarter of the input size; these were upsampled to every pixel and compared with the label using cross-entropy. All weights, encoder included, were updated. For a fair comparison, each model got ten trials to tune learning rate and weight decay on the validation set; the best setting was then retrained with ten random seeds and the test scores averaged.

Landslides used a simpler decoder. The 128 × 128 Sentinel-2 chips (10 m) were reduced to the six HLS-equivalent bands and upsampled to 224 × 224. Four transposed convolutions each doubled the 14 × 14 token grid back to 224 × 224, and two convolutions turned the result into landslide and background scores; for the 600M model, whose grid is 16 × 16, a final bilinear resize fixed the size. Landslide pixels are rare, so the loss either weighted them four times more heavily than background (weighted cross-entropy, 2 : 8) or optimised overlap directly (Lovász loss). The whole experiment was repeated with only 50 training images to test label efficiency.

What the paper found

How it was trained and run

Burn severity and US crops feed three dates at once. For burn severity these are before, during and after the fire; for crops, three points in the growing season. The encoder sees all 3 × 14 × 14 = 588 tokens together, so attention can compare each place across dates. Its output is laid back on the grid with a time axis, the three dates are merged into one feature map (the paper does not say how), and a decoder assigns a class to every pixel. Burn severity used LoRA, an UPerNet decoder and a weighted loss because high-severity pixels are rare, with a higher learning rate for the decoder than for the adapted encoder. US crops used full fine-tuning and a simpler decoder of convolution and upsampling blocks.

PASTIS has 38–61 irregular dates per sample, too many to use directly. The authors split each sequence into 20 equal time windows and picked one image from each, which keeps the whole season. With 128 × 128 chips, 20 dates × 8 × 8 patches gives 1,280 tokens. Stacking 20 dates of features into the decoder would make it very large, so a lightweight temporal attention encoder (LTAE) first learns how much each date matters at each location and an aggregator combines them into one feature map before UPerNet. All foundation models were fine-tuned with LoRA in the same way, with all labels and with 10%.

One label per image: land cover and crop type with few labels

Sen4Map gives a 12-month Sentinel-2 series around each European field-survey point. The paper cropped 15 × 15 pixels around the point and upscaled to 224 × 224, so the patch size did not have to change. Move the slider to see how each model holds up as labels are removed.

Weighted F1 from Tables X and XI. The pre-trained models lose less as labels shrink: at 6.25% of the land-cover labels, Prithvi-EO-2.0-600M is 7.3 points above the ViViT baseline trained from scratch, against 3.4 points with all labels.

How it was trained and run

This is a classification task: each sample is a 64 × 64 Sentinel-2 chip around one surveyed point, labelled with one land-cover class and one crop type. Only the centre matters, so the authors cropped 15 × 15 pixels around the point. That is about one patch, and rather than shrink the patch size, which would mean retraining the patch embedding, they upscaled the crop to 224 × 224. Twelve monthly composites form the time series, giving 12 × 196 = 2,352 tokens. The encoder's output tokens are pooled into a single vector and a linear layer predicts the class. Training was repeated on smaller and smaller shares of the labels, down to 6.25%, and compared with a video transformer (ViViT) trained from scratch on the same data.

R4

A value per pixel: forest biomass

BioMassters asks for above-ground biomass per pixel in Finnish forests, from a year of Sentinel-1 radar and Sentinel-2 optical images. Prithvi-EO-2.0-300M used a convolutional decoder with a regression head, trained with LoRA. Lower RMSE is better.

Why optical images saturate (illustrative)

Twelve dates beat four, even though pre-training used four. Adding radar as extra input bands made results worse, probably because radar is physically unlike the optical data the first layer was trained on. The authors suggest a separate radar encoder whose features are fused later. Optical reflectance stops rising once the canopy closes, so all models underestimate the highest biomass; radar penetrates the canopy.

How it was trained and run

Biomass is a per-pixel regression: the target is a LiDAR-derived map of tonnes of biomass per 10 m pixel over each 2.56 km tile. The authors first compared five inputs: 4 or 12 monthly images, optical only or with radar, and six or all Sentinel-2 bands. Radar was added as extra input channels to the patch embedding, which then had to learn weights for them from scratch. Twelve optical images with LoRA worked best, and that setup was retrained on 50% down to 5% of the data. A fully convolutional decoder upsamples the encoder's features back to 256 × 256, and a regression head outputs one number per pixel, scored by root-mean-square error against the LiDAR map.

R6

A value per site, with other data: photosynthesis at flux towers

Gross primary productivity (GPP) is measured at 37 eddy-covariance towers. The Prithvi encoder is frozen and turns the HLS image around each tower into an embedding. A small network turns ten MERRA-2 weather and soil variables into features. The two are concatenated and a linear layer predicts GPP. Each year is held out in turn.

R² from Table XV. The random forest and XGBoost baselines use spatial averages over 1.5 × 1.5 km, with and without vegetation indices (VIs); Prithvi uses raw bands only. A frozen encoder is the cheapest way to use Prithvi: no gradient flows through it, and its embeddings can be combined with any tabular or gridded data.

How it was trained and run

Here Prithvi is used only as a feature extractor. For each tower and day, an HLS chip centred on the tower goes through the frozen encoder, and a small decoder of two linear layers compresses the embeddings. In parallel, ten MERRA-2 weather and soil variables for that day pass through three small convolution layers and a linear layer. The two outputs are flattened and joined, and a final linear layer predicts the day's GPP. Only these small branches learn, using mean squared error. With 975 samples, the data are split by year: train on three years, test on the fourth, and rotate through all four.

Across benchmarks: GEO-Bench

On the twelve GEO-Bench datasets (six classification, six segmentation, 0.1 m to 15 m pixels), the 600M models with and without metadata had the best combined score, about 8% above Prithvi-EO-1.0. DOFA matched them on classification. Three patterns stand out:

  • Data beats size alone: a 100M model with Prithvi-EO-1.0's architecture, pre-trained on the new global dataset, scored about 3% higher than the original.
  • Metadata helps overall, even on datasets that provide no dates or locations.
  • Resolution transfers: despite 30 m pre-training, the models did well on drone and aerial tasks such as tree crowns and cattle.

Every model had ten hyperparameter trials per dataset and ten repeated runs; images were resized to 224 × 224 and given only the bands each model was pre-trained on.

Strengths and weaknesses seen in the experiments

Strengths

  • Label efficiency. The gap over baselines widens as labels shrink: landslides with 50 images, 67.0 vs 59.7 mIoU (U-Net) and F1 49.7 vs 35.0; Sen4Map at 6.25% of labels, 62.3 vs 55.0 F1.
  • Real temporal modelling. Handles 12 and 20 dates though pre-trained on 4; on PASTIS it beats the non-temporal foundation models DOFA and Satlas by 16–22 mIoU points.
  • Scale and global data pay off. Same architecture, new data: +3% on GEO-Bench; bigger models are usually better; metadata embeddings help on average.
  • Transfers across sensors and resolutions after fine-tuning: Sentinel-2, aerial and drone imagery.
  • Cheap to adapt. Every downstream experiment ran on one GPU; LoRA and frozen-encoder setups worked; weights and TerraTorch configurations are open.
  • Good embeddings. Frozen 600M-TL embeddings gave the best GPP results (mean R² 0.81 vs 0.75 for a ResNet with the same inputs).

Weaknesses

  • Coarse tokens. A token covers 480 m at 30 m pixels. Fragmented, small targets suffer: burn-severity classes 1–4 reach only 10–27% IoU, and the authors blame the large patch size.
  • Not always better than a specialist. Biomass RMSE 33.4 vs 27.5 for the challenge-winning U-Net; on PASTIS the 300M model (51.6) trails U-TAE (52.9) and the 600M leads by only 0.5.
  • Optical only. Radar added as extra bands made biomass worse; other modalities need a separate encoder and fusion.
  • Headline gains are modest on easy classes. Flood mIoU rose only from 88.3 to 90.3; the real gains are in the minority class.
  • Bigger is not always better. The 300M model won landslides with full data and matched the 600M on Sen4Map crop types; the 600M without metadata had the lowest GPP R² in 2018.
  • Costs and gaps. Pre-training took 21,000–58,000 GPU-hours. Inference cost grows with dates and image size, since attention is quadratic in tokens. Masking choices were not ablated, and most downstream tests are in North America and Europe, with only one Indian site (Kodagu, in the landslide data).
