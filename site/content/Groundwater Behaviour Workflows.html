Groundwater Behaviour Workflows
Catchment groundwater · workflow design

From catchment layers to groundwater behaviour

Two workflows for diagnosing which groundwater behaviour scenario operates in a catchment. The first assumes several geo-coded wells with multi-year monthly or seasonal depth-to-water records. The second assumes only one or two wells, supplemented by structured recall from farmers, and ends in extraction limits and warnings by rainfall class.

What the workflows take from Cuthbert (2010)

Cuthbert, M. O. (2010). An improved time series approach for estimating groundwater recharge from groundwater level fluctuations. Water Resources Research, 46, W09515. doi:10.1029/2009WR008572

Water-table fluctuation

Recharge is storage change plus drainage away from the well. The hard part is the drainage term D.

q = SY·∂h/∂t + D
Drainage far from rivers

Away from connected drainage, D barely varies in time and tracks the long-run mean head (eq. 10 for thick aquifers, eq. 12 when T varies with h).

D ≈ 2h̄T/(L²−x²)  |  K(H̄²−b²)/(L²−x²)
Position matters

Head swings are largest near the divide and damped near the river, where D follows recharge and the method fails. Eq. 8 or Table 1 tells you which wells qualify.

A = |qa·cosh(λx)/cosh(λL)|, λ² = iωSY/T
Geometry, sensitivity, inversion

L ≈ half the catchment area over total river length. T, L and river level h₀ set mean recharge; SY sets its amplitude. With recharge known, the method inverts to give aquifer parameters.

L ≈ Acatch / (2·ΣLriver)
Beyond the paper

The paper's model is linear and never fills or empties. The storage-capped, floor-limited and depletion scenarios below extend it for shallow hard-rock aquifers and heavy pumping.

Workflow A

Several monitored wells, multi-year records

Goal: identify the prevalent behaviour scenario, calibrate a matching model, and map heads, depth to water and flow direction.

Prepare and classify the well records

Cuthbert steps 2–4: position, sampling resolution, representativeness, pumping

Inputs
Monthly / seasonal depth to water, several yearsWell depth and type (dug / bore)
Compute
  • Convert depth to head: h = zground − DTW. Treat "dry" readings as censored values below the well bottom.
  • Flag readings affected by pumping, and possible confined behaviour.
  • Classify each well as near-drainage, mid-slope or near-divide using eq. 8 (does A ≪ D hold?).
  • Check that sampling resolves the expected swing given unsaturated thickness (Hydrus-1D where unclear).
  • Keep dug and bore wells apart when they tap different layers.

4

Extract behaviour signatures

Per well, then across wells using their distances to drainage

Per well
  • Seasonal rise ΔH (post- minus pre-monsoon) against monsoon rainfall: linear, or flattening at high rainfall.
  • Ceiling test: annual peak level steadier than rainfall; peak near ground or the aquifer top.
  • Floor test: dry-season minima clipped at a repeated level; dry readings.
  • Dry-season recession constant τ; lag to rainfall by cross-correlation.
  • Memory: autocorrelation of annual levels; dependence on the previous year's rain.
  • Trend in pre-monsoon level (Mann–Kendall, Sen slope) set against the rainfall trend.
  • Drought drop and number of years to recover; response to heavy-rain events.
Across wells
  • Mean head above drainage against d(2L−d): the slope estimates q/(2T) (eq. 9).
  • Amplitude and phase against distance to drainage: fitting eq. 5 gives diffusivity T/SY.
  • Repeat with each stream order as the boundary; the best-fitting order is the effective constant-head drainage.
  • Spatial coherence of anomalies between wells.
h̄(d) = q·d(2L−d)/(2T),  with d = L − x

Signature vector per wellq/T and T/SY estimatesEffective drainage order

Infer the prevalent scenario

Match record signatures to six candidate scenarios, S1–S6. B5 uses the same six, read from farmer recall.

Sketches show water level (up = shallower) over six years. Shaded: drought year, then heavy-rain year.

Fit the candidate model for each scenario, and select using out-of-sample error on hold-out years (including the drought and heavy-rain years) together with the signature evidence. Report a probability per scenario, not a single label. Scenarios can co-occur: S6 can sit on top of any other, and S2 with S3 (fills every monsoon, empties every summer) is common in shallow hard-rock aquifers. The markers show how reliably records detect each scenario; B5 compares this with recall.

6

Calibrate the matching model

Cuthbert steps 5–7: estimate, test sensitivity, corroborate by a second method

Model options
  • Diffusive scenario: modified WTF with eq. 13 (thick) or eq. 12 (thin), giving a recharge series.
  • Inversion: with qpot as known recharge, solve for T and SY.
  • Transfer-function noise models per well, with rainfall, ET and pumping stresses (for example Pastas).
  • Threshold bucket model for storage-capped or floor-limited scenarios.
  • 2-D groundwater model (MODFLOW) where wells are dense enough.
qt = SY(ht−ht−Δt)/Δt + 2h̄T/(L²−x²)
Checks
  • Sensitivity: T, L and h₀ shift mean recharge; SY scales its amplitude (Table 2).
  • Compare the WTF recharge with qpot from the soil-water balance; persistent gaps point to pumping, lateral inflow or a wrong SY.
  • Negative WTF recharge in dry years signals abstraction (as in the paper's 1995–96 example).
  • Monte Carlo over the priors for uncertainty; validate on hold-out wells and years.
Produces
T, SY, b, effective LRecharge series with uncertaintyAbstraction estimate

Produce maps

Physics-guided interpolation, then derived layers

Method
  • Head surface = drainage bed elevation plus the mound term q·d(2L−d)/(2T) in each micro-watershed.
  • Krige the well residuals around that surface, with height above drainage and distance to drainage as drift terms.
  • Depth to water = DEM − head surface.
Map set
  • Depth to water: pre- and post-monsoon, plus drought-year and wet-year versions.
  • Flow direction from the head gradient, checked against micro-watershed connectivity.
  • Recharge (mean, drought, wet) and storage change.
  • Saturation-prone (waterlogging) zones and dry-season stress zones.
  • Aquifer-thickness hints, and an uncertainty map.
Validate
Hold-out wellsBaseflow at gaugesGRACE regional trend

2

Read what the available wells can and cannot say

Data-availability scenarios, by position relative to drainage

Case A

No monitored well

Usable for
Nothing directly. Rely on recall and priors.
Consequence
Lowest confidence. Prioritise one tape-measure reading per season in a dug well far from drainage.
Case B

Wells close to higher-order drainage

Usable for
Drainage base level h₀, seasonal stream connection (gaining or losing), damped amplitude.
Not usable for
Recharge. D follows q near the boundary, so WTF fails (eq. 8).
Case C

Wells far from drainage, near the divide

Usable for
WTF recharge with D ≈ qa; largest swings; clearest ceiling, floor and trend signals.
Caveat
Recharge scales with the SY prior; local patchiness can bias it.
Case D

One near and one far well

Usable for
A two-point mound gives a rough q/T; the amplitude ratio gives a rough T/SY.
Caveat
Only valid if both wells tap the same layer and lie in the same micro-watershed.

Each case sets which recall questions carry the most weight. In Case B, recall from wells far from drainage fills the gap. In Case C, recall near drainage adds the base level. Wherever an instrumented well overlaps recall years, use it to measure recall bias.

3

Run a structured recall survey

Geo-tag every well, so its distance to each drainage order is known

Sampling
  • Stratify by distance to drainage (near, mid, far), lithology, well type and depth.
  • Start with about 20–40 wells per micro-watershed, enough for several per stratum; adjust to the variability found.
  • Record answers in local units (feet, haath, rings in a dug well) and convert later.
  • Ask about named years from the event calendar, not "a dry year".

5

Diagnose the scenario from recall signatures

The same six scenarios as A5, read from recall instead of records. P(scenario) ∝ prior × likelihood of the observed signatures.

Report the top two scenarios with their probabilities, and the single extra measurement that would best separate them. For example, an end-of-monsoon tape reading in a far-from-drainage dug well separates S2 (fills to the top) from S5 (long memory). Scenarios can co-occur, as in A5. Because the labels match, a diagnosis made from recall can later be confirmed against records.

Records versus recall

Records are better at

  • Timing, lags and slow trends over many years
  • Exact amplitudes, and fits against distance to drainage
  • Separating rainfall-driven change from other change

Recall is better at

  • Extreme events: overflow, wells going dry
  • Human actions: deepening, new borewells, pumping hours
  • Covering many wells, including heavily pumped ones that monitoring avoids

Both are weak at

  • Telling local geology from local pumping as the cause of differences between neighbours (S4 versus S6)
  • Any scenario whose evidence lies outside the years covered

6

Fit a minimal water-balance model per micro-watershed

A store between a floor and a ceiling

Structure V = SY·Au·(h − hfloor), capacity SY·Au·(hceil − hfloor)
  • Inflow: qpot(t)·Au, capped by the room left; the excess becomes runoff.
  • Outflow: baseflow k·V, plus abstraction Qabs, plus domestic use Qdom.
Calibrate
  • Fit SY, hfloor, hceil and k to the instrumented series (hard constraints) and to the recall intervals (soft constraints).
  • Abstraction from pump hours × discharge, cross-checked against irrigated area × crop water requirement.
  • Validate on the named drought and heavy-rain years.
