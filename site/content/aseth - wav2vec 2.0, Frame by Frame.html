The two phases at a glance

Pre-training uses audio alone: the model hides parts of its own input and learns to identify what was hidden. Fine-tuning reuses the encoder and Transformer, drops the quantizer, and adds one output layer trained against transcripts. Click a box to jump to its stage below.

The quantizer's two 320-entry codebooks start as random vectors and are learned during pre-training, through the contrastive and diversity losses. They are thrown away for fine-tuning. At test time, a separate text-only language model can help choose between candidate transcripts (stage 07).

01

Feature encoder: waveform to frames

Pre-training and fine-tuning

The input is the raw waveform: one number per sample. Seven convolutional layers slide over it, each one halving or fifthing the time resolution. The result is one vector z every 20 ms, each summarising a 25 ms window of sound. The outlined box on the waveform is the window the followed frame sees.

In the real model each z has 512 numbers learned by training. Here we show 24 band energies as a stand-in, which is roughly what early layers of such encoders end up detecting. Bright means more energy in that frequency band.

02

Quantizer: snapping frames to speech units

Pre-training only

Each z is also sent to the quantizer, which picks one entry from each of two codebooks of 320 entries. The pair of choices names a discrete unit, and a linear layer turns the two picked vectors into the target q. A Gumbel softmax makes the picking trainable: random noise is added to the scores, and a temperature τ controls how decisive the choice is. Training starts at τ = 2 and anneals to 0.5.

The unit chosen at every frame (same colour = same unit)

The units here are hand-assigned so you can see the pattern a trained model tends to show: the vowel in “cat” and the vowel in “sat” reuse the same units, and the start, middle and end of a sound often get different units. In the real model nobody assigns them; they emerge, and they line up only loosely with phonemes. A diversity loss pushes the model to use all 2 × 320 entries.

03

Masking: hiding spans before the Transformer

Pre-training

A fraction p of frames is drawn as span starts, and the M frames from each start are hidden. Each hidden z is replaced by one shared, learned mask vector. The quantizer still sees the original z, so the correct answers are kept. With p = 0.065 and M = 10, about half the frames end up hidden, in spans of about 300 ms, roughly a syllable.

With only 44 frames the masked share jumps around from draw to draw. The real model trains on 15–20 s crops (750–1,000 frames), where the share settles near the expected value 1 − (1 − p)M. A span of one 20 ms frame would be easy to guess from its neighbours, which is why whole syllables are hidden.

04

Transformer: building context

Pre-training and fine-tuning

The Transformer turns each z into a context vector c. At every layer, each frame looks at all other frames and decides how much to borrow from each: that is self-attention. The bars show how much the followed frame attends to every other frame at the chosen layer. Masked frames carry only the mask vector, so they have little to offer and get less attention.

What a probe can read from the vectors

A probe is a small classifier trained afterwards to read which sound a vector encodes. For a masked frame, z is just the mask vector, so the probe can only guess. As context accumulates through the layers, c recovers the hidden sound from its surroundings. Order information comes from a convolution over neighbouring frames that is added to the input, rather than from fixed position codes.

Attention patterns and probe readouts here are illustrative, following trends reported in layer-by-layer analyses of wav2vec 2.0: early layers look locally, middle layers gather word-sized context, and phonetic information is strongest in the middle-to-upper layers, while the very last layers become specialised for the pre-training task. BASE has 12 layers of 768 numbers per frame; LARGE has 24 layers of 1,024.

05

Contrastive loss: pick the true unit

Pre-training only

For each masked frame, the model compares its context vector c with the true target q and with distractors: the targets of other masked frames in the same clip (100 of them in the paper). Similarity is measured by cosine similarity, divided by κ = 0.1, and turned into probabilities with a softmax. The loss is −log of the probability given to the true target. Drag the slider to see training push the true target up.

Distractors come from the same speaker and recording, so voice quality and microphone cannot give the answer away: the model must encode which sound occurred. A distractor that happens to be exactly the same unit as the target would be an impossible test, so the implementation drops it (shown struck through). Near-identical units, like another part of the same sound, are the hard negatives that teach fine distinctions.

06

Fine-tuning: where text comes in

Fine-tuning

Text plays no part in pre-training. For fine-tuning, the quantizer is dropped, the encoder is frozen, and a new linear layer turns each context vector into scores over about 30 tokens: the letters, an apostrophe, a word-boundary token “|”, and a special blank “_”. The transcript has 11 characters but the clip has 44 frames, and nobody says which frame belongs to which letter. CTC (Connectionist Temporal Classification) solves this by letting the model emit blanks and repeats, then collapsing them.

During training, CTC adds up the probability of every frame path that collapses to the correct transcript, and the model is trained to raise that total. For the first 10,000 updates only the new output layer learns; after that the Transformer is fine-tuned too. Masking is still applied, as a regulariser, so the model does not memorise the small labelled set. To spell a double letter such as the “ll” in “hello”, the model must put a blank between the two: l _ l.

07

Decoding: a language model breaks ties

Inference

At test time a beam search keeps several candidate transcripts. Each gets a score that combines the acoustic model with a separate language model trained only on text: score = log Pacoustic + α · log PLM + β · words. The paper tunes α between 0 and 5 and β between −5 and 5. Here, “cad” fits the sound slightly better than “cat”, but is a far less likely sentence.

What is real and what is illustrative. Architecture numbers come from the paper: the encoder's kernels and strides, the 20 ms frame rate and 25 ms receptive field, 2 codebooks × 320 entries, τ annealed from 2 to 0.5, masking with p = 0.065 and M = 10, κ = 0.1, 100 distractors, frozen encoder and output-layer-only warm-up in fine-tuning. The clip, the features, the unit assignments, the attention weights, the probe readouts and the decoder scores are simulated so the mechanics are visible.
